## Overview

A publishing workflow helps you move content from draft to live documentation without losing quality or context. In Hamid Raza, you can use a simple review process to keep pages accurate, consistent, and easy to maintain.

This guide shows a practical starter-kit workflow for draft content, approvals, versioned updates, and ongoing maintenance. You can adapt it to your team size, release cadence, and content ownership model.

## Draft vs published content

Draft content is where you write, experiment, and make revisions. Published content is the version readers rely on, so it should reflect approved messaging, verified instructions, and current product behavior.

A clean separation between draft and published content reduces risk. It also makes it easier to collaborate because reviewers can focus on final quality instead of unfinished ideas.

### Recommended status model

| Status | Purpose | Visibility |
|--------|---------|------------|
| Draft | Work in progress | Internal only |
| In review | Ready for feedback | Internal only |
| Approved | Ready to publish | Internal only |
| Published | Live documentation | Public or team visible |

### Practical rule

Keep a page in draft until it meets your review checklist. If the content changes because of product updates, re-open the draft and re-validate the affected sections before publishing again.

## Review and approval steps

A lightweight approval process helps you publish safely without slowing down your team. The key is to make the same checks every time.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write the draft" icon="pen-tool">

Create the page content in your editor and keep the structure clear. Focus on what changed, why it matters, and what readers should do next.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Review accuracy" icon="search">

Check links, examples, terminology, and any product-specific instructions. Verify that the steps still match the current behavior.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Approve changes" icon="check-circle">

Have a reviewer confirm that the page is complete, readable, and ready to go live.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Publish the page" icon="rocket">

Move the approved content into the published site or release branch.

  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="info">

If your team publishes often, keep the approval checklist short. A small checklist that you actually use is better than a long one that people skip.

</Callout>

## Versioned updates

Versioned updates help you explain what changed and when. This is especially useful when readers need to compare older instructions with newer behavior.

Use version labels to group changes by release, milestone, or documentation cycle. That way, you can trace a page update back to the reason it changed.

<CodeGroup tabs="Markdown,JSON">
  ```markdown
## v1.2.0

- Updated the publishing checklist
- Added review ownership guidance
- Clarified draft and published states
  ```

  ```json
{
  "version": "v1.2.0",
  "status": "published",
  "updatedBy": "Documentation Team",
  "updatedAt": "2026-01-15"
}
  ```
</CodeGroup>

### Update practices

- Use a clear version label for each meaningful revision.
- Include the reason for the change in the release note.
- Keep older versions available when readers may still depend on them.
- Retire outdated instructions once the new process is stable.

## Content maintenance

Publishing does not end when a page goes live. You should review content regularly to keep it useful and correct.

A maintenance routine is easier when you assign ownership. One person can own the page, while another person reviews critical changes before publication.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Weekly checks" icon="calendar">
    Review high-traffic pages, broken links, and any content that references recent product changes.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Monthly checks" icon="clipboard-check">
    Audit core pages for consistency, outdated screenshots, and missing context.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Release checks" icon="package">
    Re-validate affected pages before and after product releases so documentation stays in sync.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

### Maintenance checklist

1. Confirm the page still matches the product behavior.
2. Replace outdated examples with current ones.
3. Remove temporary notes and internal placeholders.
4. Verify internal links and cross-references.
5. Re-run the approval process for major changes.

<Expandable title="What should you update first?" default-open="true">

Start with the sections that affect reader trust: installation steps, output examples, version numbers, and troubleshooting notes. These tend to become outdated first.

</Expandable>

<Expandable title="How do you handle minor edits?" default-open="false">

For small wording fixes, you can use a lighter review path as long as the change does not affect meaning, steps, or safety. If in doubt, route the edit through the full review flow.

</Expandable>

## Suggested workflow summary

1. Draft the content.
2. Review it for accuracy and completeness.
3. Approve it with a second set of eyes.
4. Publish the final version.
5. Revisit the page on a regular schedule.

This workflow keeps your documentation predictable and easy to maintain. It also gives you a simple process you can customize as your documentation program grows.
